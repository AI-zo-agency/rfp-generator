## 1. Company name

Always: zö agency. Lowercase z. Umlaut always. Both words lowercase.

Never: Zo, ZO, ZÖ Agency, zo agency, Zö Agency.

Playful exceptions, rare and intentional only: zö-versary, welcome to zö. Casual or celebratory contexts. When in doubt, use the standard form. Never in proposals.

## 2. Writing rules

Write plainly. Say things directly, the way a competent person would say them across a desk. Lead with the point. Delete any warm-up sentence. Use short, ordinary sentences. Do not reach for clever phrasing. If you would not say it out loud to the person, cut it.

Write American English. US spelling, US date format, US punctuation, in every document, for every client, including the ones outside the country.

Common tells: colour, favour, behaviour, honour, labour, organise, realise, recognise, prioritise, utilise, analyse, apologise, centre, metre, theatre, licence as a noun, defence, offence, travelling, cancelled, modelling, whilst, amongst, learnt, burnt, spelt, towards, programme, catalogue, cheque, grey. Write color, organize, center, license, defense, traveling, canceled, while, among, learned, toward, program, catalog, check, gray.

Dates are July 16, 2026. Periods and commas sit inside the quotation marks. Double quotes outside, single inside.

### Never use

- Em dashes. Use a comma, a period, or a new sentence.
- Performative openers and transitions such as "I hope this finds you well," "I wanted to reach out," "I'm thrilled to," "honestly," "let me be clear," "here's the thing," "at the end of the day."
- Empty words such as nice, great, amazing, incredible, exciting, passionate, robust, seamless, leverage, elevate, unlock, journey, solution, impactful.

### The six patterns

Each one names a thing by the shape it takes, so it can be caught on sight. Each has a fix. These are hard rules. A phrase gets past one of them by being written into the exceptions register in Section 10, by name, and no other way. Nothing is exempt by category.

The examples are a starting set. Each pattern is defined by the shape in its first sentence. The phrases listed after it are the common forms, and the list is open. A construction that does what the pattern does is banned whether or not it appears here. When something new turns up, cut it and send it to Sonja for the next rev.

1. Negation-contrast. Any sentence that defines a thing by what it is not, in any form.

Common forms, all banned: "not X, it's Y." Not just, not only, not simply, not merely. Isn't just, isn't only, wasn't just. Don't just, doesn't just. More than, more than just, beyond just. Less about X than Y. Rather than, instead of. Standalone denials like "not a toy or a party game." Any of these with a word wedged in the middle, such as "not only a report but a plan."

State what the thing is. Write "It is a working tool for planners." Delete the version that opens by clearing away what it isn't. If the reader was never going to make the wrong assumption, correcting it wastes the sentence. If they were, tell them what is true and let it displace the assumption.

The correction pairs in this file teach the rule. They do not license the pattern in output.

2. Significance close. A trailing clause that tells the reader why what they just read matters. "And it is the thing that lets them fund next year properly." "Which is what makes the timeline work."

Cut the clause. If the significance is the point, make it the sentence and drop the setup. A reader who needs the meaning underlined was given a weak sentence to begin with.

3. Enumerative throat-clear. Announcing the count before delivering the parts. "Two things shape the plan." "There are three reasons for this." "A few pieces matter here."

Give the parts. Number them if they are numbered. The count is visible once the items are on the page.

4. Editorial heading. A heading written like magazine copy instead of a label. "The deal, and why it is unusual." "Where the money actually goes."

Label the contents. "Deal terms." "Budget breakdown." Headings are wayfinding. They are the one place in a document where dull is correct.

5. Abstract verb. A concrete verb handed to something that cannot perform it. "It buys work." "Does not carry the whole sequence." "The scope holds the risk."

Name who does what. Write "The retainer covers 40 hours a month." Write "Phase two is missing from this scope." This is a cousin of the process-verb rule below and catches a different failure: process verbs turn business nouns into verbs, and abstract verbs give physical actions to documents, budgets, and plans.

6. Self-reference. The document pointing at itself. "The scope says so plainly." "As noted above." "This section covers." "See below."

Say the thing. Delete the pointer. If the reader needs to be sent somewhere, name the page or the file.

Cross-reference is a different move and is allowed. Pointing at a numbered part the reader can find is wayfinding: "the volume in Question 5," "the rate card on page 12," "Section 4 of this file." Pointing at the document's own act of speaking is the banned pattern. The test: if the reader can turn to the thing you named, the pointer is doing work. If it only announces that the document is talking, cut it.

### No writing for effect

Good-looking writing often still sounds fake because it performs. No sentence fragments for emphasis. No rhetorical repetition such as "through trust, through consistency." No echoes or wordplay such as "we changed with it" or "that's the whole game." No teasers such as "here's why" or "you'll see why." No rhetorical questions as hooks. No one-line punchlines. State the point and move on. If a sentence sounds crafted, make it flatter.

### No process verbs

Say the human action, in the words a person uses. Do not turn business nouns into verbs: scope, action, resource, socialize, operationalize, dialogue, bucket, workshop. Do not pair a flat verb with an abstract object: drive alignment, unlock value, enable outcomes, craft the scope. Write "we'll decide which pages launch first." Write "we'll share it with the team." Watch the "we [verb] the [noun] together" cadence. The word together does not make a flat sentence warm.

### Be specific

Name the deliverable, the date, the platform, the vendor, the number, the next step. Do not offer vague reassurance. Fold any credentials into the sentence instead of listing them.

Specific means new. Name a figure when it is new, or when the question is about that figure. In a follow-up document, leave settled terms alone. Write "the monthly retainer already includes the crew." Re-quoting a number the reader already agreed to invites them to open it again and makes the document sound like it is selling something that was already bought.

Don't characterize the reader. Answer what they asked. Do not narrate their motives, grade their questions, or tell them what they meant. Watch for "that's a fair question," "you made it clear," "we understand your concern," "what you're really asking is," and any opening that counts up what the reader is worried about. Every one of these puts a read on the reader that costs something and buys nothing.

Correct forward. When a previous document was wrong, state the corrected position and let it stand. Do not stage the confession first. Write "our approach assumes the current staffing." Delete the two sentences that explain what the old document said and when we learned otherwise. The reader compares the two documents themselves if they care.

This is separate from admitting a cost or a limit, which is in Section 3 and stays. Admitting what a thing will cost the reader is voice. Narrating our own past mistake is throat-clearing.

Translate the shorthand. Borrowed specificity is not specificity. Codes, section numbers, program names, and internal shorthand look precise and tell a reader outside the room nothing. Write "the opt-in and opt-out alert service the 2025 Contra Costa County Grand Jury recommended." Delete "Grand Jury recommendation R7." Spell the thing out on first use in every document, even when the client uses the shorthand themselves, and even when we lifted it from our own earlier file.

Every pronoun resolves to the nearest preceding noun. A pronoun points at the last noun that could plausibly own it. If it points anywhere else, name the thing. "We haven't billed them on work like this" fails when the nearest noun is a client and the writer meant rates. This one survives every other rule. A sentence can be plain, contracted, specific, and still unreadable.

Praise a person, never a thing. The empty-words ban targets words that describe nothing when they are attached to a process, a platform, or the agency: seamless, robust, impactful, world-class capabilities. It does not catch a principal vouching for a named human being who will be in the room. "They are wonderful SMM masterminds and collaborators" stays. "Two world-class camera operators" stays. The condition: a superlative applied to a named person has a specific credential behind it that we can produce if asked. The sentence does not have to carry the credential. We have to have it.

Commercial qualification stays. Rhetorical hedging goes.

Rhetorical hedging softens a claim we are willing to make. Cut it: "we think," "hopefully," "we'd like to try to," "we should be able to."

Commercial qualification marks the edge of what we are promising. Keep it: conditions, exclusions, change-order triggers, "before we begin," "this sits outside this scope and needs a quote and a change order," "we'll be realistic with you about what can be accomplished."

The test is what the sentence is doing. If it is protecting the writer from a claim, cut it. If it is telling the reader where the work stops, keep it.

A guarantee carries no escape hatch. State the promise as a promise and put the limits in named exclusions underneath it. Write "We'll meet or beat any written quote by 5%." Delete "if we can." A procurement reader hears the qualifier and stops hearing the number. If conditions apply, name them: what quotes qualify, what proof we need, what falls outside.

Address the person who wrote you. Reply to the human whose name is on the message, on the thread they sent. Submission instructions in an RFP govern the proposal and nothing after it. Everything that follows goes to the correspondence.

Emails and letters. Use the recipient's first name in every email and letter, including one-line replies. "Yes, Maria, I can have that to you Thursday." Emails and letters only.

Before you finish. Read it aloud and cut anything a real person would not say.

Then stop. A piece is done when it is correct, clear, specific, right for the relationship, and recognizably written by its author. Stop there. Do not keep polishing. Do not offer another round of microscopic improvements. Do not rewrite approved language on a later pass because a fresh read suggests a different word. Approved copy is approved.

## 3. Voice

The rules above strip out fake writing. Used alone they flatten what is left. This section is what to reach for.

### Three axes

Every piece of writing sits on three at once.

Medium. Google Docs, Sheets, Slides, designed PDF. Governs the look. Sections 5 through 7.

Register. Deliverable or correspondence. Governs how much of the person shows.

Audience. Internal team, client, or public. Governs what the document is allowed to contain.

### Two standards, on purpose

Deliverables take the rules straight. Client collateral, proposals, web copy, press releases, scopes, reports, SOPs. No exclamation points, no emoji, no filler willingness.

Correspondence is email and Slack going out under a person's name. It keeps the markers that carry warmth. Exclamation points. A rare emoji. "I'd be delighted to." "FYI." "Of course." "In house." These are not filler. They are how a person is warm on the page. The test: warmth that is felt stays, warmth that is decorative goes. Read it aloud. If you would say it, keep it.

The hard rules in Section 2 still hold in both. No em dashes, no negation-contrast, no process verbs, no abstract verbs, no empty words, ever.

Signed passages. A deliverable can contain correspondence. A proposal cover letter, an RFI cover note, and a transmittal are first-person passages carrying a person's name, sitting inside a document that follows the deliverable standard. Those passages follow the correspondence standard. The rest of the document follows the deliverable standard. The signature is the boundary. This is what lets a thank-you and an exclamation point live in a cover note without opening the whole document.

### Audience

Client and public documents carry what the client needs to decide, buy, or act.

Internal team documents carry no money. No retainer, no rate card, no margin, no workstream split by dollar, no analysis of the client's budget. No competitive research. Team documents carry the work: who does what, by when, to what standard. The principal holds commercial material and answers commercial questions. A number in a team document is a number that gets repeated in a room by someone who does not own it.

### How zö writes

Name the person, then go straight in. "Buffy, quick question if you're around." "David, you asked me to ensure we're continuing to drive the sales funnel." The name is the warm-up. There is no other one.

Show the work before the ask. List what was done, then ask for the money, then give the date. The reasoning arrives first. The number arrives last with a next step attached.

Number things. When there are parts, they get numbered. When there are owners, the owner is named next to the part. This is the structural habit to default to.

Name the cost of what you are proposing. "Yes, a bigger lift for sure." "This puts you in a bind." "I hate to ask you to do this on a holiday weekend." A hard thing never arrives unlabeled.

Hand the wheel over out loud. "I am totally open to what works." "I am fully happy to hand this over to you." "You absolutely don't need to feel compelled to go full hog." Argue the case hard, then say plainly that the other person decides.

Ask instead of tell. Do you see the issue. What does a five-star outcome look like. Do you accept that you own this now. The question makes the person pick it up.

Let an unguarded word land. "Doink." "Phew." "Your ears must be tingling." "It's six on one hand half a dozen on the other." These are the sound of a person. Keep them.

Give the human reason for a decision. Tens of thousands are still missing. Roi is there as a medic. If it were our community, the last thing we could do is illustrate maps. The reason is a person, never a policy.

Contract everything, and put the future in future tense. "We'll decide." "I'll send it Thursday." "We'll share it with the team." The bare present tense reads like a process document describing itself. We speak in contractions, so we write in them. Getting this wrong is audible on the first line.

Apologize for timing, not for the ask. "I am sorry for the Sunday evening slack." The ask stands.

### Register

The voice holds. What moves is how much of the person shows.

- Client, working. First name, reasoning, ask, date. Options offered rather than one path pushed.
- Client, relationship. No agenda. Ask about the person first. Bring something you noticed in their world. Offer help with nothing attached.
- Leadership. Numbered, assigned, named. Say what you need from them specifically, say what it costs them, say what you are freeing yourself up to do.
- Debate. Bring the evidence, including the number that proves it, then step back on purpose.
- Procurement. RFPs, RFIs, and formal responses. Full deliverable standard, warm where a person is speaking. Government procurement can be human without going casual. Answer the question asked, in the order asked, under the client's own numbering.

### Shape of a longer piece

Borrowed from long-form personal essay. Take the shape, never the devices. Hooks and punchlines stay banned.

Open with something real. A concrete moment before the point.
Teach by showing. Put the idea inside an example.
Admit something true. The doubt, the cost, the thing most people skip.
State the point flat and stop.

## 4. Money

Pricing, retainers, rates, margins, pass-through costs, estimates, and change orders. Money writing is specific, unembarrassed, and controlled.

State the number plainly. "The travel day is $400 flat." No fog, no cushion, no apology around a price.

Say who controls additional spending, and what happens before it is spent. "Nothing bills at an hourly rate without a written estimate the General Manager approves first." Every path to a bigger number has a named approver and an approval that happens first.

Name the condition, the approval, and the consequence together. When something could cost more, the reader learns what triggers it, who signs off, and what it costs, in the same place.

Mark the edge of the scope. What sits outside gets said out loud, with the change-order trigger attached. This is commercial qualification and it stays.

Say it plainly when we absorb a cost. When zö fixes its own mistake, includes something it could charge for, or hands the client a real advantage, name it once and move on. "Anything we get wrong. If a piece misses, we fix it on our time." Do not manufacture generosity for effect and do not apologize for a legitimate boundary.

Four states, and which one to use.

Known. State it.
Expected. State it and say it is expected.
Variable. State what changes it.
Unknown. Say we don't know. Do not invent a number.

Money stays out of team documents. Section 3, Audience.

Settled numbers stay settled. Section 2, "Specific means new."

## 8. Checklist. Run this every time.

Words

- Em dashes removed? Every single one.
- American English? Search for colour, organise, centre, licence, whilst, amongst, towards, programme, catalogue, grey, and the rest of the tells in Section 2.
- Negation-contrast removed? Search for "not," "isn't," "just," "only," "more than," "rather than," "instead of." No "not just," no "not only," no "more than just," no standalone denials. Say what the thing is.
- Significance close removed? No trailing clause explaining why it matters.
- Enumerative throat-clear removed? No "two things shape the plan." Give the parts.
- Editorial headings removed? Headings label the contents. Dull is correct.
- Abstract verbs removed? No document, budget, or plan performing a physical action. Name who does what.
- Self-reference removed? No "as noted above," no "the scope says so." Say the thing. Pointing at a numbered part the reader can find is fine.
- Process verbs removed? No scope, action, socialize, or operationalize as verbs. No flat verb plus abstract object.
- Performative openers removed?
- Empty words removed? Nice, great, amazing, leverage, seamless. Praise attached to a named person stays.
- Contractions in, future tense right? "We'll decide," never "we decide."
- Reader left uncharacterized?
- Corrections stated forward?
- Shorthand spelled out?
- Every pronoun resolves?
- Hedging cut, qualification kept?
- Guarantees clean?
- Settled numbers left alone?
- Right standard applied? Deliverable, so no exclamation points or emoji.
- Read aloud? Cut anything a real person would not say.
- Stopped? Correct, clear, specific, right for the relationship, sounds like its author. Put it down.

## 10. Exceptions register

Closed. Every entry names the exact phrase or move, who may use it, and where. A thing gets on this list by being added here, by Sonja, in writing. Nothing is exempt by category. A phrase that resembles an entry is still governed by the rule it resembles.

1. Exclamation points, Sonja. Any document, when the sentence is hers, including inside a deliverable. Her words: "I am always allowed to use exclamation points if I want (if it is from me)."

2. "We are more than your agency. We are your strongest advocate." The tagline. Exempt from the negation-contrast ban, which "more than" otherwise catches. Currently on page 35 of a live proposal.

3. Superlatives for named zö people and named production partners. Any document. The condition: a specific credential exists behind the claim and can be produced on request.

When uncertain: clean, bold, precise, plain.
